ROAS vs MER vs ROI: which number should you actually report?

"What's our return on ad spend?" sounds like a simple question. But ROAS, MER, and ROI are three different measurements, and mixing them up is one of the most common ways marketing reports mislead the people reading them.

The three definitions

  • ROAS (return on ad spend) = revenue attributed to a channel or campaign ÷ spend on that channel or campaign.
  • MER (marketing efficiency ratio) = total revenue ÷ total marketing spend. It is a blended number with no attribution model involved.
  • ROI (return on investment) = (profit generated − cost) ÷ cost. It requires margin, not just revenue.

A worked example

These numbers are hypothetical, for illustration only. Say a campaign spends $100,000 and is credited with $400,000 in revenue.

  • ROAS = $400,000 ÷ $100,000 = 4.0x. That looks excellent.
  • If the product carries a 30% gross margin, that revenue produced $120,000 of gross profit.
  • ROI = ($120,000 − $100,000) ÷ $100,000 = 20%. Still positive, but far thinner than 4.0x suggests.

Your break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ margin. At a 30% margin that's about 3.3x. A campaign running at 3.0x ROAS is losing money even though the dashboard shows green.

When to use each

  • ROAS: comparing campaigns, creatives, and audiences within a channel, where the attribution method is consistent.
  • MER: the executive view of overall efficiency. Because it ignores attribution, it can't be inflated by two platforms claiming the same sale, and it holds up when tracking is imperfect.
  • ROI: investment decisions such as whether to scale a channel, cut it, or shift budget to another one. This is the number finance cares about.

Common pitfalls

  • Comparing ROAS across channels that use different attribution rules. A platform that gets credit for view-through conversions will look better than one that doesn't, whether or not it performs better.
  • Letting branded search flatter the average. Branded terms capture demand that already existed, so blending them with prospecting campaigns hides what the prospecting is really returning.
  • Ignoring incrementality. Attributed revenue is not the same as revenue the ad caused. Holdout tests and media mix modeling help close that gap.
ROAS tells you if the ad worked. MER tells you if the business is efficient. ROI tells you if it was worth it.
